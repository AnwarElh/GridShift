There is a remake of *Knights of the Old Republic*. It was announced in **September 2021**, halted in
**July 2022**, handed to **Saber Interactive**, rebuilt from scratch across roughly three years, and
in **March 2026** its studio's chief creative officer said only: *"Yes, it is still in development.
That's all I can say."*

No gameplay has ever been shown. There is no release window. So the only version of this game anyone
can review is the one from 2003 — which is the version you can still buy, and which is what this
review is about.

## The structure is why it survived

*KOTOR*'s reputation rests on one structural decision, and it is worth being precise about what makes
it good rather than just calling it a famous twist.

The game does not add information at its midpoint. It **reframes information you already have**.
Everything you did in the first half remains exactly as it was; what changes is what it meant. That
is a much harder trick than a reveal, because it requires the first half to be written twice —
once to read one way, once to read another — without either reading feeling like a setup.

Nearly everything else follows from it. The companions argue with your choices rather than approving
them, which makes the alignment system feel like a relationship rather than a meter. The light-side
and dark-side options are legible without being a slider you optimise, because the companions supply
consequences the numbers do not.

And the game is **short by modern standards**. Twenty-five to thirty hours rather than a hundred.
That flatters its pacing enormously, and it is a reason it is easier to recommend in 2026 than most
of its descendants.

## Combat is the part that did not survive

*KOTOR* runs on a turn-based d20 system — dice rolls, attack bonuses, rounds — presented in real
time with a pause. It is neither an action game nor a visible tactics game, and the seam shows.

The practical result is that combat looks like it should reward timing and does not. What it rewards
is character-sheet decisions made an hour earlier. Players who understand that enjoy it; players who
do not read it as watching two figures swing at each other while numbers happen elsewhere.

This is not a flaw the passage of time introduced. It was a compromise in 2003, made so that a
d20-derived rules set could be sold to an audience that expected *Star Wars* to look like *Star
Wars*. It is simply more visible now that the surrounding conventions have moved on.

## The second half is rushed, and always was

The first half is dense, varied and well-paced. The second half compresses. Planets get shorter,
choices get thinner, and the closing stretch resolves faster than the setup earned.

This is well documented and was noted at release. It matters for a revisit because it is the part a
remake would have the clearest mandate to fix — and the part no remake has ever been described as
fixing, because no remake has been described at all.

## What it looks like now

It looks like 2003. Screenshots understate this; motion makes it plain. The animation set is stiff,
the faces are limited, and the interface is a 2003 interface — dense, small, and built for a
resolution nobody uses.

That last point matters more than the fidelity. Old graphics are a style you adjust to in twenty
minutes. An old interface is friction you meet on every single interaction, for thirty hours.

## Where the score comes from

**8.4** is a game with a top-tier structure, a genuinely good cast, and two systems — combat and
interface — that a modern player has to actively forgive. The structure carries it. The presentation
is what keeps it off a higher number.

It also explains, better than any rumour does, why the remake exists as an idea. The valuable part of
*KOTOR* — the structure, the writing, the companions — is entirely portable to a new engine. The
weak part — the combat model and the interface — is exactly what a remake would replace. It is close
to an ideal remake candidate on paper.

Which is the frustrating part. On paper is where it has stayed for five years.
