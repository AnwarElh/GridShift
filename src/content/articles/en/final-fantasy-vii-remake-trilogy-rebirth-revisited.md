*Final Fantasy VII Rebirth* has an unusual problem for a game this well made: it cannot be finished
in the sense that matters. It ends, but it does not conclude. Two years after release, with the
finale now dated for **8 April 2027**, that is no longer a complaint about a cliffhanger — it is the
central fact you have to judge it on.

## What it does better than Remake

Almost everything mechanical.

**Combat** is the clearest case. *Remake* introduced a hybrid of real-time action and menu-driven
ATB commands that worked but felt like a proposition. *Rebirth* treats it as settled and builds:
synergy abilities tie characters together so that party composition becomes a moment-to-moment
decision rather than a menu screen. Crucially it does this **without discarding what you already
learned** — a rare piece of restraint in a sequel.

**The cast** is where the game spends its ambition. The relationships are not only written; they are
tracked, and they alter what you see. Making affinity a mechanical fact rather than a cutscene
variable is the reason the game's most discussed moments land for different people in different
ways.

**The world** is dense enough to justify leaving the critical path, which is the only test an open
world has to pass.

And **Queen's Blood** is a genuinely good card game. Not good *for a minigame* — good.

## The minigame problem, stated fairly

It is the most divisive thing about *Rebirth*, and both sides are right about different things.

The volume is real. The critical path is interrupted often, and several interruptions are mandatory.
For a player who wants the story, the pacing suffers in a way that is hard to defend across a
hundred-hour game.

The quality is also real. The best of them are well-made and optional, and a game confident enough
to build a working card game inside itself is doing something most of its peers would not attempt.

What makes this more than an old argument is that **Square Enix has publicly treated it as a problem
to solve**. Director Naoki Hamaguchi has spoken about resolving what he called the minigame issue,
and the stated approach for *Revelation* has two halves: returning minigames such as Queen's Blood
come back in enhanced form, and material from later in the 1997 original — the Great Glacier
snowboarding, for instance — is being reimagined to connect to the narrative rather than sit beside
it. That is a design position, not a denial.

## The ending is the review

*Rebirth* ends on a withholding. Not an accident of scope — a deliberate decision to leave the
trilogy's central question open for a third game.

Judged as a middle chapter, that is defensible and arguably correct: the middle volume of a trilogy
that resolved its own premise would have nothing for the third to do. Judged as a purchase, it means
something plainer. **You cannot start here, and you cannot finish here.** The game requires *Remake*
before it and *Revelation* after it to become the thing it is arguing to be.

That is a real cost, and it is why the score is not higher. A game that depends on two other games
to complete its own argument is asking for a commitment most releases do not.

## What has changed since 2024

Three things, and they all improve the case for playing it now rather than then.

**It is no longer PlayStation-and-PC only.** *Remake Intergrade* and *Rebirth* both reached **Switch
2 and Xbox Series X|S during 2026**, with *Rebirth* landing on Switch 2 on **3 June**. Starting the
trilogy no longer requires owning a specific console.

**The finale has a date.** 8 April 2027, confirmed at Sony's State of Play on 3 September. The wait
is now finite and known, which is a materially different proposition from an open-ended one.

**Your save will matter.** A *Rebirth* save grants the Phoenix Summon Materia in *Revelation*, and a
*Remake Intergrade* save grants the Chocobo and Moogle set. They are conveniences rather than
decisive advantages, but they are a reason not to delete anything.

Worth stating plainly for context rather than as reassurance: Square Enix was internally
disappointed with *Rebirth*'s sales on PS5 and Windows. Hamaguchi has addressed the concern publicly,
saying the finale would be a proper, high-quality send-off. That is the developer's position, offered
here as the developer's position.

## Where the score comes from

**8.9** is a game that improves on its predecessor in every system, spends a hundred hours earning a
conclusion, and then declines to deliver one. The systems justify a higher number. The structural
dependency does not allow it.

The honest version of this review is that its final score is not fully payable until April 2027. We
will re-check it then, because that is when the thing *Rebirth* is actually doing becomes visible.
