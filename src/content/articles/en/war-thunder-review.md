There is a thing War Thunder does that no other free-to-play game does, and it is worth stating
before the complaints, because the complaints are what everyone leads with.

## The damage model is the product

When a shell hits a tank in War Thunder, the game resolves where it struck, at what angle, what it
passed through, what spall it produced, which modules that spall reached and which crew members it
killed. A hit is not a number subtracted from a health bar; it is an event with a diagram. You can
lose a gunner and keep fighting, or lose a fuel tank and burn out, or bounce a round off a mantlet
you should have avoided and learn something.

Aircraft get the same treatment — control surfaces, engine cooling, individual fuel tanks — and so do
ships. Thirteen years of this, across roughly a hundred years of military hardware, in one client.
Nothing else in the genre approaches it, and June's Heavy Cavalry update showed the studio still
extending it across all three domains simultaneously rather than settling into one.

## Realistic Battles is the game

Arcade exists and Simulator exists, but Realistic is where War Thunder's identity is. No enemy
markers except what you can see and what your team calls; ballistics that require you to range a
shot; aircraft that stall if you fly them badly. A ground Realistic match is fifteen minutes of
looking very hard at a treeline, and it produces tension almost nothing else does.

The learning curve is close to vertical and the game does nothing to soften it. There is no
meaningful tutorial for the concepts that decide matches — angling armour, spotting, ammunition
choice, when a shot is worth taking. New players lose for a long time without being told why.

## And then the economy

You cannot review this game honestly without it. Progression runs on Research Points and Silver
Lions; vehicles cost both to unlock and Silver Lions to repair after every match. At top rank, repair
costs have historically been high enough that a bad game costs more than a good one earns — which
means the reward for climbing the tree is a vehicle you cannot afford to play badly in.

This is not an abstract grievance. It produced one of the largest organised player protests in the
medium, and the changes that followed came after that pressure rather than before it. Gaijin has
moved on the numbers more than once since. The structure — free-to-play, grind-gated, with premium
vehicles and premium accounts that measurably change the pace — has not.

Battle Rating compression compounds it. The matchmaker places you against vehicles up to a full
rating above yours, and at several points in the tree that gap is the difference between competitive
and irrelevant. Learning which rating brackets are worth playing is a real part of the skill, and
learning it means losing.

## Who should install it

Anyone who wants the simulation. If the appeal is the hardware — reading an armour profile, judging
a deflection shot, nursing a damaged airframe home — this is the only place that gets it right at
this scale, and it is free.

Anyone who wants a fair progression curve should know what they are agreeing to first. Play the
mid-tier brackets, where the vehicles are interesting and the economy is survivable, and treat top
rank as somewhere to visit rather than a destination.

7.5 is a high score with a large asterisk, and after thirteen years the asterisk is not going to be
patched out. It gets revised when the economy does.
