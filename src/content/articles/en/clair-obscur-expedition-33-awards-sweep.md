Awards seasons in games are usually a negotiation. One show gives the big prize to the enormous
thing, another gives it to the difficult thing, the developers' vote goes somewhere else entirely,
and by March there are four defensible Games of the Year and an argument. The 2025–26 season did not
do that.

## The numbers, laid out

- **The Game Awards 2025:** 13 nominations, **9 wins** — the most wins in the show's history —
  including Game of the Year.
- **DICE Awards 2026 (29th):** 8 nominations, **5 wins**, including Game of the Year.
- **GDC Awards 2026 (26th):** Game of the Year, plus Best Debut, Best Visual Art, Best Narrative and
  Best Audio, at the March 12, 2026 ceremony. Nominated in nearly every category.
- **BAFTA Games Awards 2026 (22nd):** led the field with **12 nominations**, tied for most wins with
  three, including Best Game.
- **Sales:** 500,000 in 24 hours, 1 million in three days, 2 million in twelve, 3.3 million in
  thirty-three, and **8 million as of April 2026**.

Sandfall Interactive's first game. Published by Kepler Interactive. Released April 24, 2025 on PS5,
Windows and Xbox Series X/S, day one on Game Pass.

## Four juries is the part that matters

Take any single one of those results away and you have a normal story. A big night at The Game
Awards is a press-and-public phenomenon. A BAFTA is a British academy. DICE is the industry
congratulating itself in a ballroom. The GDC Awards are voted on by developers, which is a
different constituency again — people who know what it costs to build the thing they are voting for.

The interesting fact is not that Clair Obscur won a lot. It is that four bodies with genuinely
different incentives, different voter pools and different criteria arrived at the same answer, with
no meaningful dissent between them. That is not how this normally goes.

When it does happen, it usually means the field agreed on something more specific than "this was
good". The categories give a hint at what: debut, visual art, narrative, audio. Those are craft
categories, and they are the ones where a smaller team can outperform a larger budget if the
direction is coherent enough.

## A debut studio is not supposed to be able to do this

This is where the story stops being pleasant.

The distribution of prizes in this industry usually tracks the distribution of money, because the
things awards measure — production values, scope, polish, marketing reach that gets a game in front
of jurors at all — mostly track money. A first game from a studio nobody had heard of, published by
a comparatively small publisher, is not supposed to lead a BAFTA field with twelve nominations.

That it did should be read two ways at once. The generous reading is that the system works: an
excellent game found its audience and the juries did their job. The less generous reading — the one
worth sitting with — is that a debut clearing the bar this comprehensively means the bar was not
where the industry's spending suggests it was. Nine trophies did not go to any of the very large,
very expensive games in that cycle. Somebody at each of those studios has had to explain that.

The eight million copies matter here too. This was not a critics' darling that nobody bought. Being
on Game Pass day one is part of that reach, and it is worth noting that day-one subscription
availability did not stop the game selling eight million copies — which is an argument several
publishers have spent years making the other way round.

## The Musée d'Orsay is the strangest line on the CV

There is an exhibition at the Musée d'Orsay tied to this game.

Games get museum shows occasionally, usually in institutions that collect design or moving image,
usually framed as "video games as culture" with a curator explaining the medium to a sceptical
audience. The Musée d'Orsay is a nineteenth-century art museum. It did not need a games angle and
was not looking for one.

It is the only item in this whole run that did not come from inside the industry. Every award listed
above was voted on by people who work in or write about games. The Orsay is a different kind of
recognition entirely, and it is the one that will still be legible in twenty years.

## What the sweep does not prove

A few things worth holding on to, because the trophy list has a way of ending conversations.

**It does not prove the game is beyond criticism.** The free Thank You Update's Endless Tower is
built to a difficulty that Kotaku described as curb stomping even the game's best players, and
Arabic language support did not arrive until June 30, 2026 — fourteen months after launch, taking
the total to twenty languages. Both of those are real, and neither is in the citation.

**It does not prove the model is repeatable.** One debut studio winning everything is a data point,
not a strategy. There is no announced next Sandfall game, and the second one will be made under
completely different conditions from the first — which is the condition under which most studios
that win everything make their worst work.

**It does not prove anything about the games that lost.** A sweep compresses a whole year into a
single verdict, and years do not actually work like that. Nine awards is a measurement of agreement,
which is a real and rare thing. It is not a measurement of quality with the arguing removed.

What the sweep does prove is narrower and more useful: that in 2025 a first-time French studio,
working with a mid-size publisher, made a game that the press, the industry, the developers and a
British academy all independently decided was the best thing anyone did that year. Everything else —
including what it means — is still open.
