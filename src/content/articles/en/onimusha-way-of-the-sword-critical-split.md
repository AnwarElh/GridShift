*Onimusha: Way of the Sword* has been out since the start of the month, and the critical response
has not converged the way it usually does by week two. **Kotaku**'s review, published August 31,
summarises the game as Capcom burying something special under a lot of compromises. We scored it
**8.2**. Both of those can be true, and reading them side by side shows why.

## What nobody disagrees about

The boss fights. Kotaku's review is unambiguous on this point and spends real space on it: the
duels are where *Way of the Sword* reaches its peaks, they get brutally difficult, and they are
built so that no single timing window is impossible — the game asks for consistency rather than
perfection, and Capcom's sound team makes each correct read land.

Its comparison is not to *Sekiro* but to *Punch-Out!!* — learn the animation, learn the response,
repeat until the opponent cannot touch you. That is a compliment, and it matches what we found.

## Where the review turns

Kotaku's case against the game is not about combat feel. It is about how much game surrounds it.

The argument runs: *Onimusha*'s identity was the **Issen**, the perfectly-timed counter, sitting
inside a tight, interconnected space with deliberate movement and a focused resource economy.
*Way of the Sword* keeps the Issen and replaces the rest — an open world instead of a connected
map, heavy lock-on and attack magnetism instead of careful spacing, and several overlapping
currencies instead of one meaningful decision about souls.

The consequence, in that reading, shows up in mob fights rather than boss fights. Musashi's
moveset is built around single-target parries, so groups take turns attacking to keep it viable.
Issens are locked animations now, so you cannot herd enemies and counter several at once the way
the 2001 game let you. Encounters stop being about composition and become a queue.

And then the open world multiplies that by twenty-five hours. Kotaku's sharpest line of argument
is that the original had repeatable content too — it was called replaying the game, and it was
four hours long.

## Why we still landed at 8.2

Because the weighting is genuinely arguable, and it depends on how you play.

If the duels are the reason you are here and the traversal is the tax, the game delivers the
duels at a standard very few things this year match. If you experience an open world as an
obligation to complete, the same design reads as padding around a good combat system, and the
score falls.

That is not two critics disagreeing about quality. It is two critics disagreeing about what
fraction of twenty-five hours is spent doing the thing the game is best at — which is a question
about the player as much as the game.

## The one thing to read before you buy

Kotaku's review is worth your time in full even if you disagree with its conclusion, because its
account of what the series used to be is the most precise we have seen anywhere. If its
description of the original's design sounds like the thing you actually want, that is a real
signal, and our 8.2 should not talk you out of it.
