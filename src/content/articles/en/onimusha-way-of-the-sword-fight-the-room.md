Most guides for a parry game are about timing. This one is not. Onimusha: Way of the Sword is largely
linear, which is usually filed under "less freedom" and almost never under what it actually means:
somebody designed every space you will fight in, knowing exactly what was going to walk into it. The
rooms are arguments. Reading them is worth more than another ten frames of parry window.

## Linear is a promise about the room

An open-world action game cannot compose encounters, because it does not know where you will be
standing. A linear one can. Way of the Sword uses that: the corridors, the elevation changes, the
temple courtyards with a single approach — these are not set dressing between fights, they are the
fights' terms.

Practical version: when a space opens up in front of you and it is conspicuously shaped — a raised
walkway, a bottleneck, a room with one entrance and a lot of floor — you are looking at the encounter
before it has started. Decide where you want to be standing *now*, while nothing is attacking.

## Oni Vision is a pre-fight tool

Oni Vision reveals enemy positions. Nearly every new player uses it mid-fight, panicking, and gets
almost nothing from it — in a fight, the enemy you cannot see is rarely the one killing you.

Use it on the threshold instead. Before you walk into a space, Oni Vision tells you the count, the
spread, and crucially whether anything is above you or behind the obvious line of approach. That
turns an ambush into a plan. The information is the same either way; the value of it collapses the
moment the first sword swings.

## Pick the ground before you pick the target

The instinct in a crowd is to choose a target. The better instinct is to choose a floor.

- **Take the choke.** A doorway, a bridge, a stairwell — anything that makes a group approach you in
  ones and twos. This game's combat is excellent one-on-one and merely good three-on-one, so convert
  the second situation into the first with geometry rather than with skill.
- **Put your back to something solid**, not because of flanking damage but because it stops you
  drifting. Losing a fight to a slow retreat into an open room is the most common bad death here.
- **Stand where the archers are not.** Projectiles can be deflected, and deflecting them is fun, but
  every deflection is a parry you did not spend on the sword in front of you.

The prolonged parry that steers an enemy is the tool that makes all of this active rather than
passive — you do not just choose the ground, you can move bodies onto it. If you have not got that
input in your hands yet, it is the prerequisite for everything in this section.

## The table is a real answer

You can flip a table and use it as a temporary shield. It reads as a joke the first time and it is
not: it buys you a defended angle for a few seconds without spending a parry, and a few seconds is
often exactly the gap between a boss's dangerous phase and its recovery.

The general rule the table teaches is that the environment is in the move list. Interactive scenery
is sparse in this game rather than everywhere — it is not a physics playground — so treat each piece
you find as a single-use item you have been handed for this specific encounter, because that is
usually what it is.

## Order the crowd instead of surviving it

The mistake in group fights is treating them as a health-bar problem. They are a sequencing problem.

Work out which enemy dictates the tempo — usually the one with the fastest approach or the one whose
attack forces you to move — and remove it first, even if a slower, tankier enemy is closer. Everything
else in the group is manageable at your own pace; the tempo-setter is what stops you from having a
pace at all.

Then remember that stamina damage carries: you do not have to finish an enemy you have started. If
you have worked one down to the edge of a Break Issen and another opens up, banking the execution and
switching is fine. The gauge does not reset because you looked away.

## When the room is the problem

There is one situation where all of this stops applying, and it is worth naming honestly: the open
areas. When the game stops being a corridor and becomes a small hub with optional objectives, the
encounter design goes flat, enemies spawn on flat ground with nothing to use, and there is nothing to
read.

Fight those the boring way. Guard more, take the fights one at a time, spend your Oni Armaments
freely because souls are plentiful out there, and get back to the main path — that is where the game
is doing its actual work.
