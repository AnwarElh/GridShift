Reviewing *The Witcher 3* in 2026 is a strange assignment. The game has sold **more than 65 million
copies** and collected **over 250 game of the year awards**. Nobody needs to be told it is good. The
useful question is narrower: what is actually there when you install it today, and which parts of it
is CD PROJEKT RED about to change on **29 September**?

## What eleven years did not touch

The writing. That is the whole answer, and it is why the game outlasted its generation.

*The Witcher 3*'s reputation rests on a specific, unusual achievement: its **side quests are written
to the standard most games reserve for the main story**. A contract to kill a monster turns out to be
a story about a village protecting a person it should have protected earlier. A haunted house is a
domestic tragedy. The quests resolve badly more often than cleanly, and the game rarely tells you
which ending you earned was the good one — frequently because there wasn't one.

That is a design choice, not a mood. A game whose choices resolve cleanly teaches you to optimise
them. One that resolves them badly teaches you to think about them. Eleven years of open-world
design has not produced many imitators, because the expensive part is not the branching — it is
writing two hundred hours of consequence that nobody is obliged to see.

The world holds up for a related reason: it is built to be walked through rather than cleared.
Velen, Skellige and Novigrad each have a distinct visual and cultural grammar, and the map's
points of interest are dense enough that leaving the road is usually rewarded and occasionally
punished.

## What has aged, plainly

**Combat.** It is adequate. Geralt has two swords, five Signs, and a dodge, and the fights are
readable, but the system never reaches the level of the writing it sits inside. Target acquisition
picks the wrong enemy at close range often enough that players learned to fight backing away. Group
fights resolve into circling. It is a third of what you do in a two-hundred-hour game, and it is the
part its own defenders concede first.

**Movement.** Geralt carries a deliberate weight, and the delay between input and step is a design
decision that a decade of players have never stopped complaining about. In tight interiors it reads
as unresponsiveness rather than heft.

**The skill system.** You unlock far more than you can equip. The limited active slots mean levelling
subtracts options as often as it adds them — you finish a session with more things you cannot use.
It is the least defensible system in the game.

Notably, all three are on CD PROJEKT RED's own list for the remaster: **refined targeting**, **new
gap-closing animations**, **improved traversal and movement**, and a **skill tree revamp**. The
studio has named the same three complaints this review makes. Whether it fixes them is a question
for 29 September; that it identified them correctly is not in doubt.

## The expansions are now the value proposition

This is the part that changed last month, and it changes the buying advice.

**Every current owner of *The Witcher 3* now has *Hearts of Stone* and *Blood and Wine* at no
cost**, effective from the gamescom announcement. They were paid expansions for eleven years.

*Blood and Wine* in particular is not a bonus. It is a thirty-plus hour campaign in Toussaint, a
region with its own art direction, its own tonal register and an ending that functions as the real
conclusion to Geralt's story. A large number of people who own the base game have never seen it,
because it sat behind a separate purchase. It is now simply in the library.

Reviewing the game without them would have been reviewing two thirds of it. With them included by
default, the thing you install today is substantially larger than the thing that reviewed well in
2015.

## Where the score comes from

**9.2** is the score of a game with the best writing in its genre, a world that justifies its size,
and two systems — combat and movement — that never rose to meet either. It is not a perfect game
and it never was. It is the most complete one of its kind that anyone has shipped, and the fact that
its own studio is spending a remaster attacking the same weaknesses this review names is the clearest
evidence that they are real.

## Should you wait three weeks?

If you own it: yes, wait. The upgrade is **free** on PS5, Xbox Series X|S, Steam, GOG and the Epic
Games Store, and starting a two-hundred-hour game three weeks before a substantial visual and combat
overhaul is a poor use of the two hundred hours. Claim the expansions now, start on the 29th.

If you do not own it: there is no version of this game that is a bad purchase, but the same logic
applies. Three weeks is short.

If you are buying on Switch 2 or Battle.net: those are new platforms with no upgrade path, and no
performance information has been published for either. That is a different decision, and we have
written it up separately.
