Almost every open-world survival game dies the same death. A server fills, players build, the map
fills with abandoned bases, the population drains, and what is left is a museum. Once Human's answer
is to schedule the ending.

## The scenario structure is the whole idea

You join a scenario — a server running a specific ruleset for a fixed period of weeks. You land with
nothing, you build, you progress a technology tree, you fight the season's bosses. Then the scenario
ends, the world resets, and you choose a new one.

Stated flatly it sounds punitive, and the first time it happens it feels that way. What it buys is a
world that is never a graveyard: everyone around you is on the same clock, at roughly the same stage,
with the same reasons to co-operate. The mid-game of a survival server — normally the point at which
the population has quietly halved — is the part of Once Human that works best.

It also means the game can run scenarios with different rules. PvE, PvP, harder resource economies,
different map behaviour. The reset is what makes variation possible.

## What actually carries over

This is the part the game explains worst and the part that decides whether you stay. Your levels,
your base and your gear are seasonal. Your **blueprints** — the formulas for weapons and equipment —
are permanent, as is **Eternaland**, a private space you can move a limited quantity of materials and
constructions into before a season closes.

So the meta-progression is not power; it is *options*. Each scenario you finish leaves you with more
things you know how to make next time. A returning player is not stronger at hour one than a new one.
They have a better plan.

Whether that is enough compensation for losing a base you spent forty hours on is the honest question
of this review, and the answer depends entirely on whether you build to keep or build to use.

## Deviations are the best thing in it

Deviations are the anomalous creatures you capture and install in your base, where they work.
One purifies water. One cooks. One patrols and attacks intruders. One generates power. They have
needs, they can be unhappy, and they interact with the base layout.

The effect is that a base stops being a storage room with walls and becomes a small system you are
tuning. It is the most original mechanic in the survival genre in several years, and it is doing
something no crafting tree does — giving the building phase an ongoing behaviour rather than a
finished state.

## Where it is ordinary

The shooting is fine. Guns feel adequate, enemies behave predictably outside of boss encounters, and
a large fraction of your hours are spent in combat that never becomes the reason you are playing.
In a game whose ideas are this good, competent combat is the most visible compromise.

And the blueprints run through a gacha. Weapon and gear formulas are pulled rather than found, which
puts the game's most permanent progression axis behind a random draw with a purchasable currency
attached. It is not aggressive by the standards of the market. It is closer to the core loop than a
game with this much design confidence needed to put it.

## The console version and what is next

The August 2026 launch on PS5 and Xbox Series is not a straight port: it added a first-person
perspective that can be toggled freely, alongside full cross-play and cross-progression with PC and
mobile. First-person materially changes a survival game built third-person, and it is a better
default than the original camera for interior work.

The Isles of Abyss expansion arrives in October with ship building and combat at sea — a second axis
of play rather than a seasonal event. This score is written before it, and it will be re-checked
after.

7.8 is the score of a game with two genuinely new ideas, one ordinary shooting model, and a
structural gamble it mostly wins.
