The remarkable thing about HEAT is what is not in it. There is no tech tree. There is no line of
vehicles to research before the interesting ones appear, no grind separating a new account from the
game everyone else is playing. For a studio that built a twenty-year business on exactly that
structure, deleting it is not a design tweak. It is a statement.

## What replaced it

You pick an Agent. The Agent belongs to one of three roles — Defender, Assault, Marksman — and the
role determines which tanks you can bring and what your team needs from you. Each Agent has a unique
skill and a passive trait.

Defender holds space: survivable, punishing at close range, slow. Assault is speed and flanks,
closing openings before the enemy line resets. Marksman is range, information and patience.

The roles are legible in a way World of Tanks classes never quite were, because they are stated
rather than emergent. A new player understands within three matches what their job is, which is
something two decades of tank-class conventions never managed to communicate. That clarity is the
game's biggest win.

## The habits it punishes

If you have played World of Tanks, HEAT will punish you specifically. The instinct to pick a vehicle
first and work out your role afterwards is inverted here — the Agent comes first and the tank follows
from it. The instinct to treat a sniper position as a safe default is worse: taking the Marksman
because range looks survivable and then playing at brawling distance produces a character with the
worst attributes of both roles and the strengths of neither.

The game does not warn you about any of this. Its onboarding explains the controls and leaves the
transition entirely alone, which is a strange omission given exactly who is going to install it.

## Eight is not many

Fifteen tanks and eight Agents at launch. The tanks are the smaller problem — within a role they read
as preferences rather than decisions, and that is survivable in a game where the role is the unit of
choice.

Eight Agents is the real constraint. A role-based shooter lives on the interaction between picks:
knowing what the enemy composition can do, adjusting yours, being countered and countering back. With
eight, the space of possible team compositions is small enough that after thirty hours you have seen
most of them. There is no depth problem in the moment-to-moment shooting; there is a depth problem in
the match before the match.

This is a solvable complaint — it is solved by shipping Agents — but a launch review scores what
launched.

## The technical side is genuinely good

The new in-house engine was the risk and it has not been the problem. Performance holds across a wide
hardware range, the game is supported on Steam Deck, and it runs on GeForce NOW for anyone without
hardware at all. Cross-platform play *and* progression cover PC through both the Wargaming Game
Center and Steam, PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X|S and the cloud.

That list is more complete than most launches manage, and it means the first-install decision carries
no penalty. The one caveat is latency: cloud play on a role whose job is closing distance is a real
trade, and Assault players should prefer local hardware.

## Where it stands

HEAT is the most confident thing Wargaming has released in years, and confidence is exactly what it
needed to cut its own signature system. It is fast, clear, technically solid and free of the wall
that keeps people from recommending World of Tanks.

It also has eight Agents. 7.2 is a score for a good game with a visible ceiling, and the ceiling is
the roster. Re-check this in six months.
