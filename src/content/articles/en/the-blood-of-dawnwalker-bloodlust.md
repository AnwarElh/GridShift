Coen's failed turning left him half of two things. The game splits that across the clock: human by
day, vampire by night, with a different verb set on each side. Most guides treat that as a
convenience — do the talking bits in daylight, do the sneaking bits after dark. It is more demanding
than that, because one of the two halves has a cost attached that the game will collect whether you
were paying attention or not.

## The failure state, first

At night, Coen must feed. Animals or humans; both work.

If you ignore the bloodlust meter, he loses control and kills the nearest victim. Not a scripted
victim. **The nearest one.** That can be a quest giver. That can be a trader. And they are gone
permanently — no reload prompt, no failure screen, no second chance, just a world that is slightly
worse from then on.

This is the one system in The Blood of Dawnwalker that will take something from you without asking,
and it is worth respecting from the first night rather than the first incident. Two rules cover
almost all of it:

- **Never enter a settlement at night with the meter high.** If you are approaching a village, a camp
  or anywhere with named NPCs and bloodlust is anywhere near the top, feed before you arrive.
- **Feed on your way, not at your destination.** The countryside is full of animals. Handling
  bloodlust in transit is free; handling it inside a village is a decision about who is standing
  nearby.

## Feeding is routine, treat it as one

The reason players lose someone is almost never that they were trapped with no options. It is that
feeding is boring and they put it off, the same way you put off saving in a game with no autosave.

Make it a habit tied to a trigger rather than to the meter: feed **every time night falls**, before
you do anything else with the night. It costs you very little, it is available almost everywhere
outside town, and it removes the entire risk class from your run.

Feeding on humans is also a real option with real consequences, and the game lets you make that
choice deliberately. The distinction that matters is not animal-versus-human in the abstract — it is
whether you fed **because you chose to** or because Coen made the choice for you. The first is
roleplay. The second is attrition.

## What the day is for

Daylight Coen is the one who gets to be a person.

- **NPCs are available.** Conversations, negotiations, the whole social surface of the game.
- **Non-violent resolutions are open.** A large share of quests have a route through them that does
  not involve a fight, and daylight is where those routes live.
- **Witch magic works.** Coen can contact the recently dead for clues — the game's investigative tool
  and often the fastest way through a quest that has stalled.

That last one has a lovely piece of internal logic behind it. The magic requires **runes carved into
his skin**, and his vampiric healing erases them overnight. It is day-only because his body will not
keep the marks, not because a rule says so.

Practical consequence: **if a quest looks like an investigation, do it in daylight.** Walking a murder
scene at night with claws out is not just thematically wrong, it is you having deliberately left your
best tool at home.

## What the night is for

Night Coen stops asking permission.

- **Wall-walking** and **Shadowstep**, a short teleport. Together these reach places that are simply
  not reachable in daylight — roofs, ledges, the inside of somewhere with a locked front door.
- **Claws**, and **biting for health.** Night combat has its own sustain.

So the night is for access and for force. Anywhere you looked at during the day and thought "there is
no way in", the answer is usually that you were the wrong Coen at the time.

The reverse is also true and worth planning around. There is no point arriving at a location at
midnight if what you needed was to speak to the man who owns it.

## Choosing which Coen solves a problem

This is the actual skill the game is teaching, and it is why "come back later" means something here
that it does not mean elsewhere. Later is not a delay. Later is a different toolkit.

A working habit: when you accept a quest, decide **before you travel** whether it is a day job or a
night job.

- **Day job** — anyone you need to talk to, anything that reads as an investigation, anything you
  would prefer to resolve without killing.
- **Night job** — anywhere you need to get into rather than be admitted to, anything vertical,
  anything you intend to resolve by force.

And when a quest genuinely has both, do the talking first. Information gathered in daylight tends to
open options; access gained at night tends to close them, because a place you broke into is a place
whose owner now knows something happened.

## The stealth is different at night, and harder

One trap for players carrying habits in from other games: at night you are hiding from **guards and
civilians**. Not just guards. A villager who sees Coen is a problem in his own right, and the game's
night infiltration is designed around a population that is not all armed.

Which means night stealth in a settlement is genuinely more demanding than night infiltration of a
fortress, because a fortress has fewer people in it who count. If a route through a town looks easy
at 2am, count the windows again.
