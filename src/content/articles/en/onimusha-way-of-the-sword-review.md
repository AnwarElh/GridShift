Capcom has not shipped a mainline Onimusha since *Dawn of Dreams* in 2006. Twenty years is long
enough for a genre to be invented around the corpse of your series and then to calcify. Way of the
Sword arrives into a market where every sword is a Soulslike sword, and the most interesting decision
the game makes is to walk past that entirely.

## It is not a Soulslike, and it says so out loud

Director Satoru Nihei told GameSpot in June 2025 that this is not an open world and not a Soulslike,
and the finished game is the rare case of a studio meaning it. Way of the Sword is largely linear.
It opens out periodically into areas with side quests and things to find, then closes again. It runs
about twenty hours. There is no bonfire economy, no run back to your corpse, no stat-gated wall
telling you to go and grind somewhere else.

What that buys is pacing. The game is allowed to put a fight in front of you because the fight is
good, not because the fight is a checkpoint. It is allowed to end. In a year of eighty-hour action
games apologising for their length in the second act, twenty hours that know where they are going is
a design position, and Capcom holds it.

## The parry is the whole game, and it steers

The combat is built on parry rather than dodge. You can deflect projectiles. There is a **Guard**
stance that blocks from all directions and asks for nothing clever, which is the accessibility floor
the game deliberately keeps in place. Above that floor is the mechanic the marketing never really
explained: a **prolonged parry** that does not just stop an incoming attack but *steers* the
attacker, so they stumble in a direction you choose.

That sounds like a flourish. It is not. Once you understand that you can decide where a body lands,
crowd fights change shape. You stop treating a group as a queue of individual duels and start using
one enemy as furniture to inconvenience the other three. Pushing an attacker into a doorway to buy
two seconds against the two behind him is a thought this game rewards and almost nothing else in the
genre lets you have.

Striking and parrying drain an enemy's stamina, and when it empties you get **Break Issen** — an
instant, dismembering execution. So stamina is not a bar you monitor; it is a target you are actively
aiming at, and every parry is a deposit toward it. Chain parries and dodges cleanly and the game hands
you buffs on top: chained executions, multi-hit attacks. The loop closes on itself very tightly.

The environment is in the loop too, in small unfussy ways. You can flip a table for a temporary
shield. It is not a physics playground and it does not pretend to be — it is one more verb in a fight
that already has enough.

## The gauntlet is an economy, and it is the best idea here

Musashi wears a sentient gauntlet that eats souls, and souls come in three colours. **Yellow** is
health. **Red** is upgrade currency. **Blue** charges **Oni Armaments** — high-damage special weapons
that return yellow souls when they connect.

That last clause is what makes the system work. Blue feeds a weapon that generates yellow, which
means the three currencies are not three separate meters, they are one circuit. Absorbing badly is a
decision with consequences twenty minutes later, and the game never once puts a tooltip in front of
you explaining that. You work it out because you ran out of health at the wrong moment.

The best expression of it is at bosses. Break Issen on a boss does not kill it. Instead the game asks
you to choose: take the massive damage, or take the souls. That is a real question. Massive damage is
this fight; souls are the next three. I finished the game still second-guessing that decision, which
is more than most action games manage with their entire upgrade tree.

**Oni Vision** — which reveals enemy positions — is the one part of the kit that feels bolted on. It
solves a problem the level design mostly does not have.

## Where it is ordinary

The open areas. Every time Way of the Sword stops being a corridor and becomes a small hub with
optional objectives, the quality drops a full grade. The side content is fetch-shaped, the rewards
are souls you were going to get anyway, and the game's own pacing argument stops applying to it. Cut
these entirely and the game is better and eighteen hours long.

Enemy variety runs out. By the last stretch you are fighting recognisable furniture in new arrangements,
and a combat system this readable makes repetition more visible, not less.

And the signposting. Way of the Sword is a game whose combat teaches beautifully by consequence, and
which then puts a prompt on screen to tell you the thing you just learned. The waypoint discipline is
modern-blockbuster tidy in a game that spends its best hours arguing against exactly that.

The story is the quiet disappointment. Capcom spent two years securing the rights to **Toshiro
Mifune's** likeness, and Musashi carries the game's presence entirely on that face. The writing
around him is serviceable Edo-era dark fantasy — a Kyoto choked by clouds of Malice, temples rendered
with real period consultation — and it never quite asks Mifune to do anything a lesser face could not
have done.

## The critical split is real, and both sides are right

GamesRadar called it a rough diamond, no Sekiro, carried by goofiness and accessibility. VGC's Jordan
Middler called it a series reborn with great fights and **too many wrong modern lessons**. Polygon's
preview framed it as a welcome break from the Soulslike. Those are not contradictory verdicts. They
are the same observation with different signs on it.

The accessibility — Guard stance, generous parry windows, prompts, waypoints — is what lets the
combat system be legible to someone who is not going to put forty hours into learning it. It is also
exactly what dilutes the thing that makes the combat good. Capcom made one choice and got both
outcomes. The aggregates reflect the same split: generally favourable across the board, 94% recommend
on OpenCritic, and a spread from 88 down to 82 depending on which version you look at.

## The score

**8.2.** This is a very good action game with an excellent combat system, a genuinely new resource
idea at its centre, and about four hours of open-area content that should not have shipped. It is
below the aggregate because the "wrong modern lessons" critique is correct and it costs the game
something real — the parry system is smart enough that the game did not need to keep reassuring you
about it.

It is a return worth having, and the first Onimusha in twenty years is not a nostalgia object. It is
a working idea. If Capcom makes another one and trusts the player twenty per cent more, that game is
a nine.
