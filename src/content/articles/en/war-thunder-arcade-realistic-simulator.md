War Thunder presents its modes as difficulty settings. They are not. They change what information you
receive, how the vehicles behave, and how much you earn — and a player who is unhappy in one is often
simply in the wrong one.

## Arcade: markers, and what they take away

Arcade Battles show enemy vehicles as markers, give tanks an aiming reticle that accounts for range
automatically, and let aircraft fly with simplified physics that will not stall you out of a turn.
Respawns are cheap and matches are short.

What that buys is accessibility. What it costs is the thing the game is actually about. With markers
overhead, positioning is no longer information warfare — you cannot be hidden, so you cannot be
clever about being hidden. Arcade is a shooting gallery with excellent damage modelling, and for
learning what your shells do to what armour, it is genuinely useful. As a place to live, it hollows
out the game.

## Realistic: the mode the game is designed around

No enemy markers beyond what you or your team can see. Real ballistics, so you range the shot
yourself. Aircraft that require actual energy management. One respawn per vehicle in your lineup, and
when they are gone your match is over.

This is where War Thunder's reputation comes from. A ground Realistic match is largely about not
being seen, which sounds dull and is the opposite — every decision about where to point your hull and
when to move carries a consequence you can trace. It is also where the community, the guides and the
vehicle discussion live. If you have heard people describe War Thunder as tense, this is the mode
they are describing.

The cost is patience. You will spend minutes alive and seconds fighting, and early on you will die to
things you never saw.

## Simulator: the cockpit, and the commitment

Simulator Battles remove the third-person view entirely — cockpit only in aircraft, and gunner sight
plus commander view in tanks — and use full flight and driving models with mouse-aim disabled by
default for aircraft. Matches are longer, populations are smaller, and a joystick stops being
optional in the air.

It is the mode that rewards the most study and demands the most setup. Nobody should start here, and
plenty of long-term players never leave once they arrive.

## The economics are different too

Each mode has its own reward multipliers, and Realistic pays substantially more Research Points and
Silver Lions per match than Arcade for the same performance, with Simulator higher again. Repair
costs also scale by mode.

This matters for a practical reason: a player grinding in Arcade because it is comfortable is
progressing slowly *and* learning habits that do not transfer. If you intend to end up in Realistic —
and the game's design assumes you will — the sooner you move, the less you unlearn.

## How to choose, in one question

Ask what you want from a match. If you want to shoot things and see immediate results, Arcade is
honest about being that. If you want a match where being patient and being right are rewarded more
than being fast, Realistic. If you want to fly an aircraft rather than steer one, Simulator, and buy
a stick.

Do not mix them while you are learning. The aiming, the spotting and the situational awareness are
mode-specific skills, and alternating means building none of them.
