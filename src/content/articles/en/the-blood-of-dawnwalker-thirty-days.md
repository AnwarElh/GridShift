The Blood of Dawnwalker gives Coen thirty days and nights to reach his family before Brencis is
invested as Prince. Almost every player's first instinct is the wrong one: to treat that as a timer
and to start hurrying. It is not a timer. It is a budget, and budgets reward planning rather than
speed.

## The shape of the clock

Thirty in-game days and nights. Each day is divided into **eight time segments**. That is the entire
currency, and everything you do in Vale Sangora either costs some of it or costs none of it.

Two things follow that you should internalise before you leave the opening area:

- **Nothing happens in real time.** You can stand still for an hour of wall-clock time and lose
  nothing. Put the controller down mid-quest. Read a codex entry. The world does not move without you.
- **The cost is always shown before you commit.** The game's **hourglass** tells you what a given
  activity will take. You are never taxed by surprise.

Those two properties are what separate this from every "timed" RPG that made you anxious. Dawnwalker
is not trying to rush you. It is trying to make you choose.

## What costs time

The list is shorter than you fear.

- **Plot-relevant quests.** The main thread and the substantial, story-carrying quests attached to it.
  These are the big line items, and some run to several segments each.
- **Levelling up.** Spending your progress costs segments. This is the one that catches people out,
  because in every other RPG levelling is a reward and here it is a purchase. You are trading time
  for power, and the trade is real.

That is essentially it. If an action is going to move the plot or make Coen stronger, expect to pay.

## What costs nothing

Everything else, more or less, and the list is generous:

- **Exploring the open world.** Travel, ruins, landmarks, whatever you find while walking somewhere
  else. Free.
- **Most side quests.** Optional content generally does not touch the clock at all.
- **Talking to people, shopping, poking at the world.** Free.

This is the design decision that makes the whole structure work, and it is worth saying plainly: the
game never punishes curiosity. There is no version of Dawnwalker where you regret having looked at
something. Wander as much as you like — you are only spending segments when the game has told you, in
advance, that you are.

The practical consequence: **do your exploring first.** Scout an area, find the shrines, meet the
people, take the free side content, and only then decide which of the time-consuming quests in that
region you are actually going to buy. Walking in and immediately accepting the first expensive quest
you are offered is how a first run goes badly.

## The hourglass is a price tag, read it

Every time-consuming activity shows you its cost up front. Treat that number the way you would treat
a price in a shop where your wallet does not refill.

The useful question is never "can I afford this". Early on you can afford almost anything. The
question is **"what am I not doing instead"**, and the honest answer is usually "I do not know yet",
which is why the first week is worth spending on reconnaissance rather than on commitments.

One warning about levelling specifically: because it costs segments, it is tempting to defer it
indefinitely and stay cheap. Do not. A Coen who is under-levelled for the back half loses fights, and
losing fights costs you more than the levels would have. Buy your power in the first two weeks when
segments are plentiful, not in the last week when they are not.

## You are meant to miss things

Rebel Wolves put **more time-consuming quests in the game than there are segments to spend on them.**
Deliberately. There is no route, no guide and no optimisation that gets you all of it.

This is the part players fight hardest, and fighting it is the single biggest way to have a worse
time. If you approach Dawnwalker as a completion problem you will spend fifty hours anxious about a
board you were never going to clear, and the drama the structure was built to create — this person's
problem versus that person's problem, and you can only help one — evaporates into arithmetic.

The alternative is straightforward and much better: **pick what you care about.** When two quests
compete, take the one with the character you want to see more of, not the one with the better reward.
The game is built so that answer is always defensible.

## A budget that works

Not the only way to run thirty days, but a shape that holds up:

1. **Days 1–7: reconnaissance.** Explore hard, take the free side quests, meet everyone, buy your
   early levels while segments feel abundant. Commit to few expensive quests.
2. **Days 8–20: commitment.** This is where the money goes. Choose your major threads and follow them
   properly. Resist the urge to sample everything — a half-finished expensive quest is the worst
   possible purchase.
3. **Days 21–30: close.** Finish what you started, top up levels if a fight has been going badly, and
   accept that the two or three quests you never touched are somebody else's playthrough.

Check the calendar at the start of each in-game week rather than obsessively. The game is legible
enough that a weekly review is sufficient, and staring at the clock is the fastest route to playing
it like a spreadsheet.

## The end is not the end

Finishing the main quest does not necessarily end the game. Some side content remains available
afterwards. That is worth knowing before you panic-rush the finale at day 27 — and it is another
reason the correct attitude towards the thirty days is budgeting, not sprinting.
